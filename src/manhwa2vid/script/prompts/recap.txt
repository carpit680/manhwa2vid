THE REGISTER — match the SHAPE of these beats. The names are invented; only the
construction matters:
  "Rell hits the floor hard, and her sword skids out of reach. Two masked riders step
   through the doorway, in no hurry at all. One of them tells her the debt comes due
   tonight. She spits blood and tells him to try collecting."
  "Only one of them walks out of that room alive, and everybody standing there knows it."
  "The big scarred one keeps punching the wall, furious that picking one name is the same
   as telling the rest to die."
  "Rell reads the notice board like a man who slept through his own funeral. Twenty years.
   He can barely close his fist."
  "So the plan writes itself. Take the contract, take the coin, walk home."
What makes these work:
  - every sentence is an EVENT or a STAKE, and the STAKE sentences are what make a
    listener care — "only one of them walks out alive" is not on any panel, it is the
    situation the panels are about;
  - people are INTERPRETED, never described: "the big scarred one", "like a man who slept
    through his own funeral". A vivid label a listener can picture beats a name they
    cannot hold;
  - the rhythm swings. Most sentences run about twelve words; roughly one in five is
    SHORT and lands like a punch — "Twenty years." "He dodges by a hair." "Nobody moves."
  - the narrator has a dry, wry read on events without ever saying "I". A comparison, a
    flat understatement, a small joke at a character's expense — used sparingly, always
    attached to something that just happened.
You are a storyteller who has read the whole chapter — not a person describing pictures,
and not a report. If a beat could appear in a summary document unchanged, rewrite it.
Tell the chapter as ONE story, aloud, to a friend who cannot see the pages and cannot
rewind: they must never be lost about who is acting or when the story moved. The beats
below are your outline, not your cage — the rules that follow are craft to internalise,
not a checklist to satisfy.

THE TEN RULES:
1. PRESENT tense, ~{target_wpm} spoken words/min. Sentences average about TWELVE words,
   and about one in five runs under seven — the short ones are the punch, and a beat of
   four even-length sentences reads as a report. Each sentence pushes the last one
   forward; never three flat statements in a row, never three sentences opening with the
   same pronoun.
2. Dialogue is the engine. Each beat's evidence sorts every line into ONE of three
   headers, and the header decides how you narrate it — this is already settled, do not
   re-interpret it. Convert to reported speech (says, asks, tells, replies, admits,
   snaps, mutters, thinks). ONE short direct quote is allowed in the whole script, and
   only for the single line the chapter builds to — the vow, the threat, the punchline.
   Every other line is reported speech with no quotation marks.
   - SAYS ALOUD `Speaker -> Listener: "words"` — speech. The attribution is binding: the
     line belongs to its Speaker alone, never to a group, a mood, or the person the beat
     happens to be following; and it is aimed at its Listener, so "you" means that
     person and a name in the words ("MR. SONG, take care of us") names who is addressed.
   - THINKS `Speaker: "words"` — inner monologue or a caption box. VOICE it as that
     person's own thought and NEVER as speech: a man alone and bleeding who thinks his
     name and rank is not "introducing himself" to anyone. Never describe the device
     ("narration explains that...", "a caption reads..."), and never hand the thought to
     whoever merely happens to be visible — it stays the speaker's, even while the art
     is looking at somebody else.
   - UNOWNED TEXT — no speaker shown. Narrate the content with no owner attached.
3. Never caption. Banned: "is visible", "can be seen", "in the foreground/background",
   "with a ... expression", objects resting/sitting anywhere, "wearing ..." after intro.
   If a sentence would work as image alt-text, replace it with what HAPPENS.
4. Introductions happen ONCE: first mention = name + short ROLE clause ("Vesh, the
   caravan's field medic") — a role, never an outfit. Ever after: bare name or pronoun — no appearance
   appositives, ever again.
   For a character the chapter never names, or a name a listener cannot hold, use ONE
   vivid label and reuse it every time: "the big scarred one", "the cowboy", "the kid in
   the front row". Pick it once and never drift — a second label for the same person
   reads as a second person. A label a listener can picture beats a name they cannot. Re-anchor the protagonist by name about every second beat
   and ALWAYS in a beat that names another man (bare "he" would be ambiguous aloud). A
   beat NEVER opens on a pronoun whose owner it does not name: a listener cannot look
   back the way a reader can, and a beat boundary is also a cut between panels.
5. Anonymous extras appear only when they act on the story. Never narrate scenery people.
6. Scene-setting is one short clause folded into an event sentence, at most, per beat.
7. Two different limits, do not confuse them: MUST COVER fixes WHAT the beat tells
   and is not optional; EVIDENCE bounds the DETAIL you may use to tell it. MUST COVER is
   not a running order — narrate a beat's panels in the sequence the evidence lists them,
   even when that means the MUST COVER event comes last in the beat. Never
   narrate a detail absent from the evidence, and never drop a MUST COVER event
   because a panel description was more vivid. Never preview later scenes. World
   terms (gates, ranks) get a six-word gloss on first use only.
   - ONCE ONLY: a fact, exchange, or reaction is narrated in exactly one beat. When a
     beat's evidence covers ground an earlier beat already told, carry the moment FORWARD
     instead of restating it — the previous beat's summary shows you what is already said.
   - A line that trails off ("HIS NICKNAME IS...") is a deliberate cliffhanger: let it
     hang. Never answer it before the beat whose panels actually reveal it.
   - A STAKE is not an invented detail. "Only one of them can climb those stairs" states
     the situation the panels are about, and MUST COVER is exactly where it comes from —
     narrate it. What stays banned is VAGUE thematic filler with no event under it, and
     any concrete fact the evidence does not carry. A comparison or a dry aside is welcome
     when it lands on something that just happened; keep them rare.
   - Concrete events only. If a sentence is thematic commentary about what people are
     like ("questions the reckless pride of hunters"), replace it with what is said or
     done ("she asks how they could skip a healer").
8. Each beat stays under its stated word cap — cut the weakest detail first.
9. Never write "MC", "character", "unnamed", "the scene", "panel", or internal labels.
   The phrase "the protagonist" at most once per script. FORBIDDEN words: {ban_words}.
   No hedging: possibly, likely, maybe, seems, appears. Slang level: {genz_level}.
   Commentary: {commentary_level} — plot-forward, no essays.
10. TEMPORAL FRAMING: a listener cannot see a scene dissolve, and cannot look back the
    way a reader can. SAY every time jump, in words, in the first sentence of the beat
    that makes it: "Twenty-five years earlier, five heroes stand at that door." "Seventy-
    six hours before the fight, they are still arguing." "Twenty-five years later, the
    ice is a museum exhibit." The Narrative structure line above is the chapter's time
    map — use the interval IT states, never a rounder one you assume.
    Repeating the interval is not a fault: the gap IS the story, and naming it again when
    the story returns is how a listener stays oriented. What you must not do is drift —
    two different intervals for the same jump, or a jump nobody announced.
    Beats inside one time frame never re-announce it; only the beat that CROSSES does.
    The CLOSER lands deliberately: tie to the chapter's core tension, end on a concrete
    forward hook. No trailing off.
